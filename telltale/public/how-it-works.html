Methodology

No model is judging the text.
Just measurements, weighted.

TellTale is a stylometric estimator. It counts things: sentence lengths, phrase habits, punctuation rates, pronoun density. Then it blends those counts into one probability-like score and shows you the arithmetic. There is no second AI hiding behind the curtain, and no vendor confidence number you have to take on faith.

Yes, we know. This page uses em dashes and the occasional "furthermore". That's fine — one tell in isolation means little. The signal is in the combination and the rate, which is exactly how the engine treats it.

The pipeline

Normalise & segment

Re-wrapped lines are glued back together (PDF and email pastes arrive hard-wrapped), curly quotes are straightened, then the text is split into sentences and paragraphs. The splitter is cautious: "Dr.", "3.5" and "U.S." don't end sentences.

Measure 14 signals

Each signal is a number computed from the text: coefficient of variation in sentence length, stock-phrase rate per 100 words, share of sentences opening with a discourse marker, contraction rate, em-dash rate, and so on.

Map each measurement to an AI-leaning score

A piecewise-linear mapping turns each raw value into a number in [0, 1]. Zero means the measurement looks human; one means it looks machine-made. These mappings are the editable heart of the system — the whole file is a few hundred readable lines.

Weight, blend, sharpen, shrink

Signals are combined as a weighted mean. Signals with no data (three paragraphs? no paragraph signal) sit out entirely. The middle is then slightly sharpened, and short texts are pulled back toward 50 with a confidence penalty.

The arithmetic, in full

// raw score: weighted mean of applicable signals raw = Σ(weightᵢ × aiScoreᵢ) / Σ(weightᵢ) // 0…1 // mild decisiveness curve around the midpoint adj = 50 + (raw×100 − 50) × 1.22 × confidenceFactor // confidence factor by sample size confidenceFactor = 0.55 if < 80 words = 0.82 if < 180 words = 1.00 if ≥ 180 words score = clamp(round(adj), 2, 98) // never 0 or 100

The score is deliberately capped away from 0 and 100. Nothing about authorship is certain enough to justify those numbers on a web page.

The 14 signals

Grouped by family, with the weight each one carries in the blend.

Rhythm
Sentence-length variance
weight 2.6
 Human writers swing between short jabs and long runs; the coefficient of variation is usually 0.5–0.8. Default model output clusters near its mean, giving 0.25–0.4. This is the strongest single signal in the blend.
Sentences near average
weight 1.0
 Share of sentences within ±22% of the document's average length. Only counts as human evidence when the overall rhythm actually varies — otherwise it duplicates the signal above.
Even paragraph lengths
weight 0.7
 Variation in paragraph word counts. Needs at least three paragraphs to say anything at all, otherwise it abstains.
Recycled openers
weight 1.0
 How often a sentence starts with a word that already opened an earlier sentence. Templated prose loves "The … The … The …".
Determiner-heavy openers
weight 0.5
 Share of sentences starting with The / This / These / It / There. Weak alone, useful in combination.
Vocabulary
Stock "LLM voice" phrases
weight 3.4
 A watchlist of ~150 phrases that generated prose over-uses: "delve into", "rich tapestry", "it's important to note", "in today's fast-paced world", "testament to", "cutting-edge", "moreover". Weighted by how indicative each is.
Transitional openers
weight 1.8
 Share of sentences opening with However / Furthermore / Additionally / Moreover. Human writing typically sits at 5–15%; generated essays often pass 30%.
Formal register
weight 1.3
 Density of "utilize", "comprehensive", "methodology", "subsequently" and friends. Legitimate in legal or scientific writing — the report will say so.
Long-word ratio
weight 0.5
 Share of words of nine characters or more. Noisy on its own, so it barely moves the score.
Punctuation
Em-dash habit
weight 0.9
 Em dashes per 100 words. A genuinely famous tell, but plenty of humans are devotees, and this page is proof. Hence the modest weight.
Voice
Personal voice & specifics
weight 1.3
 First-person pronouns, numbers, dates and proper nouns per 100 words. Names, prices and small true details are the hardest thing to fake and the easiest thing to trust.
Casual imperfections
weight 1.2
 Lowercase sentence starts, missing apostrophes ("dont", "im"), repeated punctuation, ellipses, emoji, double spaces. Polished to the point of sterility is itself a signal.
Contractions
weight 1.0
 don't, it's, we're, can't per 100 words. Zero contractions over a long stretch reads like documentation.
Questions & exclamations
weight 0.5
 Whether the text ever asks something or gets excited. Reference-style generation rarely does.

Register calibration

A single global scale has a well-known failure mode: careful formal writing scores high, because formality looks like machine style. TellTale answers that with register profiles. The register is detected from the text's own genre markers (methodology, participants, shall, hereby, endpoint, config, spokesperson, contractions, first-person density…) or set manually by the writer, and it changes the scoring in three controlled ways:

Weights are rescaled

Signals that legitimately differ in the genre are re-weighted: formal vocabulary counts 0.45× in academic writing, 0.30× in legal. Rhythm signals stay near full weight everywhere — no register explains away a flat rhythm and a diet of stock phrases.

Out-of-register signals are muted

Missing typos and absent contractions cannot fairly judge a contract or a technical manual. Those voice signals are muted to 0.25× in registers where they don't apply, instead of pretending their absence is evidence of a machine.

Neutral phrases are discounted

Words like "furthermore", "framework" or "comprehensive" are ordinary academic vocabulary. When the register says so, those phrase types are discounted in the watchlist contribution — while "delve into", "rich tapestry" and "unlock the full potential" are discounted in no register at all.

Every adjustment is disclosed. The report shows the score with and without the profile, the delta, which phrases were discounted, and which signals were muted. A detected register can be overridden, and a manual override is labelled assumed rather than passed off as a measurement.

Reproduce every number above with node scripts/docs-numbers.js. That last pair is the point. Calibration is not a get-out-of-jail card: it subtracts genre-normal style and nothing else.

Paragraph-level reports

Documents are rarely written by one process. A generated introduction sits on top of a human body; a human draft gets sprinkled with machine-written transitions. An overall score averages all of that into the middle and tells you nothing useful. So each paragraph is re-run through the full pipeline on its own statistics, and the document is summarised by its shape:

  • →Mixed: paragraph scores spread by 28 points or more. The report says so loudly, because the single score for that document is misleading by construction.
  • →Uniform machine / uniform human: the paragraphs agree, so the overall score is a fair summary.
  • →Even middle: inconclusive throughout. Unhelpful, but honestly so.
  • →Paragraphs under 30 words are listed but left unscored — there isn't enough rhythm inside them to measure fairly.

Evidence packs

A score inside a web page settles nothing between two people. Every report exports two ways:

Markdown report

Verdict, register and calibration, document statistics, the full signal table, the paragraph table, flagged phrases, machine-leaning passages and the suggestions — formatted for pasting into a thread, a gradebook comment or an editorial note. Endpoint: GET /api/report/:id.

JSON bundle with integrity digest

Every measurement, plus a SHA-256 digest over the payload. POST /api/verify re-computes it, so anyone can confirm the evidence hasn't been edited since it was produced. It proves the file is unmodified; it does not prove the verdict is right.

Where this breaks down

A detector is a stylostatistics engine, not a lie detector, and definitely not a judge. Using it to accuse, fail, or discipline someone is a misuse of the tool. It is evidence of style, and style is not authorship.

  • ✕Humanised or paraphrased machine text. Anything run through a rewrite pass loses the tidy rhythm that this method relies on. Paraphrased model output can score as human and there is no reliable fix for that at this layer.
  • ✕Formal human writers. Non-native English speakers writing carefully, legal drafters, academic authors and students trained on essay templates all push toward the machine side of the scale. This is a documented failure mode of every detector in this family.
  • ✕Short texts. Under ~180 words the score is pulled toward the middle for good reason: there isn't enough material to measure rhythm or habit rates. Treat anything under 80 words as noise.
  • ✕Mixed authorship. Paragraph-level reports catch most of it, but a machine paragraph edited sentence-by-sentence will still read neutral. Read the heatmap and the paragraph table together.
  • ✕Register misdetection. A legal memo with no legal vocabulary, or an academic abstract that is three sentences long, may not trip detection. Watch the register badge: weak guesses are displayed but never applied to the score.

How to read a score properly

  • ✓Start with confidence. "Low" means ignore the number.
  • ✓Then read the signal breakdown. Two strong signals agreeing is worth more than the headline score.
  • ✓Check the heatmap for mixed authorship — machine paragraphs and human paragraphs often sit side by side.
  • ✓Never make a decision about a person from one number. Get a process, a conversation, or a second opinion instead.

Try it on something you wrote

Your own writing is the best calibration test. If it flags you, the report will tell you exactly which habits did it.
